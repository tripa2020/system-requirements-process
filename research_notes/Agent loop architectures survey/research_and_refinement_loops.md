## KQ1. STORM / Co-STORM: how perspectives are discovered, how questions are generated and grounded, reported gains, and the authors' own failure modes

### Takeaway
STORM (Shao et al., NAACL 2024) discovers N perspectives by pulling the tables of contents of related Wikipedia articles and asking the LLM to name roles that would cover the topic; each perspective then drives a simulated multi-turn writer-expert conversation whose answers are grounded in filtered web search. Perspectives roughly double the number of unique sources collected and significantly improve editor-rated organisation, but the authors report that verifiability did not improve and that the dominant residual failures are "red herring" over-association between facts, inherited source bias, and lower informativeness than human articles.

### Cited Findings

**Perspective discovery (STORM §3.1).** "Given the input topic t, STORM discovers different perspectives by surveying existing articles from similar topics ... STORM prompts an LLM to generate a list of related topics and subsequently extracts the tables of contents from their corresponding Wikipedia articles ... These tables of contents are concatenated to create a context to prompt the LLM to identify N perspectives P = {p1, ..., pN}". A default "basic fact writer focusing on broadly covering the topic" perspective is always added (the N+1-th conversation). — [Shao et al., arXiv 2402.14207v2, 8 Apr 2024, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Question generation and grounding (STORM §3.2).** "In the i-th round of the conversation, the LLM-powered Wikipedia writer generates a single question q_i based on the topic t, its assigned perspective p ∈ P, and the conversation history {q1, a1, ..., q(i-1), a(i-1)} ... In practice, we limit the conversation to at most M rounds." Answers are grounded: "we first prompt the LLM to break down q_i into a set of search queries ... the searched results will be evaluated using a rule-based filter according to the Wikipedia guideline to exclude untrustworthy sources ... the LLM synthesizes the trustworthy sources to generate the answer a_i, and these sources will also be added to R for full article generation." — [Shao et al. 2024, §3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Outline then article (STORM §3.3).** "we first prompt the model to generate a draft outline O_D given only the topic t ... Subsequently, the LLM is prompted with the topic t, the draft outline O_D, and the simulated conversations {C0, ..., CN} to refine the outline." The article is then written section by section from the outline and the reference set R. — [Shao et al. 2024, §3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Hyper-parameters.** The paper states the number of perspectives N and conversation rounds M "in STORM are both set as 5" (§4, experimental setup line). — [Shao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207). The GitHub README exposes `search_top_k` and the `STORMWikiRunnerArguments` class for further configuration; the README did not list `max_conv_turn` / `max_perspective` names on the page as fetched (UNVERIFIED as exact parameter names). — [stanford-oval/storm README, latest news entry Jan 2025](https://github.com/stanford-oval/storm)

**Effect of perspectives on breadth of sourcing (Table 5).** Average unique references collected: full STORM 99.83; "w/o Perspective" 54.36; "w/o Conversation" 39.56. — [Shao et al. 2024, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Citation support (Table 4).** "Mistral 7B-Instruct judges 84.83% of the sentences are supported by their citations"; citation precision 85.18%. "the primary issues stem from drawing improper inferences and inaccurate paraphrasing, rather than hallucinating non-existent contents." — [Shao et al. 2024, §5.1 and Appendix C.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Human evaluation (Table 6; 10 experienced Wikipedia editors, 20 topic pairs, 1–7 scale, STORM vs oRAG).** Interest 3.63 → 4.03 (p=0.077); Organization 3.25 → 4.00 (p=0.005); Relevance 3.93 → 4.15 (p=0.347); Coverage 3.58 → 4.00 (p=0.084); Verifiability 3.85 → 3.80 (p=0.843); articles preferred 26 vs 14. Inter-annotator agreement was low: Krippendorff's alpha 0.221–0.388 across criteria. — [Shao et al. 2024, §6, Table 6 and footnote 11](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Editor survey.** All 10 editors agreed STORM "can be specifically helpful for my pre-writing stage" (70% somewhat agree, 30% strongly agree in Figure 3); "More reservation is expressed to the usefulness of STORM for the Wikipedia community at large; nonetheless, 70% of the editors think it is useful, with only 10% disagreeing." — [Shao et al. 2024, §6, Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**Failure modes identified by the authors (§6).** "editors assign lower Verifiability scores to articles from our approach in over 50% of the cases [where oRAG was preferred] ... low Verifiability scores stem from red herring fallacy or overspeculation issues. These arise when the generated articles introduce unverifiable connections between different pieces of information in |R| or between the information and the topic." "Generated articles trail behind well-revised human works ... editors comment that the generated articles are less informative than actual Wikipedia pages. Another major issue identified is the transfer of bias and tone from Internet sources to the generated articles." Appendix E: "the most common errors stem from the tendency of LLMs to form improper inferential links between different pieces of information presented in the context window" and "Addressing this challenge calls for high-level sensemaking rather than mere fact-level verification." — [Shao et al. 2024, §6 and Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**STORM Limitations section (verbatim excerpt).** "the quality of machine-written articles still lags behind well-revised human-authored articles, specifically in aspects of neutrality and verifiability. Although STORM discovers different perspectives in researching the given topic, the collected information may still be biased towards dominant sources on the Internet and may contain promotional content. Moreover, the verifiability issues identified in this work go beyond factual hallucination, which highlights new challenges to grounded writing systems." — [Shao et al. 2024, Limitations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207)

**README caveat.** The system "cannot produce publication-ready articles that often require a significant number of edits". — [stanford-oval/storm README](https://github.com/stanford-oval/storm)

**Co-STORM protocol (Jiang et al., EMNLP 2024).** Roles: N perspective-guided LM experts, one LM moderator, one human user. Turn management: "let different experts, p1, ..., pN, take turns in sequence. To prevent them from only expanding on the same point, upon observing L consecutive turns of expert responses with intents being either POTENTIAL ANSWER or FURTHER DETAILS, the system asks the moderator to intervene." Moderator question generation: "Co-STORM instructs the LM to generate informed questions based on the uncited sources retrieved since the last moderator turn ... the moderator reranks each piece of information i based on the similarity to the topic t and the dissimilarity to its associated question q" (score cos(i,t)^α · (1−cos(i,q))^(1−α)). A tree-structured mind map is updated by `insert` and `reorganize` operations and becomes the outline of the final cited report. — [Jiang et al., arXiv 2408.15232, full PDF, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15232)

**Co-STORM motivation and results.** Goal is surfacing "unknown unknowns". Human study with 20 users: "70% preferred Co-STORM over the search engine, and 78% preferred it over the RAG chatbot". Likert (1–5): Serendipity 2.70 (search) vs 3.90 (Co-STORM), p=0.030; Depth 3.10 vs 4.00 (p=0.081); Breadth 3.60 vs 4.10 (p=0.096); Relevance 3.90 vs 4.00 (p=0.758). Ablations: removing the moderator hurt more than reducing to one expert ("removing the moderator has a greater negative impact"); "one moderator can already provide most of the benefits". — [Jiang et al. 2024, §5, Tables 3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15232)

**Co-STORM Limitations (verbatim excerpt).** "First, the system could better tailor the collaborative discourse to the user's prior knowledge, skipping basic facts for knowledgeable users and introducing concepts progressively for novices." Plus "The primary risk of our work is the common bias issues originating from biases present on the general internet" and English-only sources. — [Jiang et al. 2024, Limitations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15232)

### Inferences
- The STORM mechanism that most closely matches the user's "typed questions from assigned roles" step is perspective-guided question asking; the measured benefit was in *coverage* (unique sources ~1.8x) and *organisation*, not in *verifiability*, which was statistically flat (p=0.843).
- STORM's dominant failure, "improper inferential links between pieces of information in the context window", is a synthesis-stage failure that question-asking does not prevent; it argues for a human verification gate between research and morphing of the document.
- Co-STORM's ablation (moderator matters more than number of experts) suggests that in a question loop the *injector of unused/uncited directions* is more valuable than adding more role-voices.

### Gaps
- Which model served which role in STORM (the paper's text indicates a chat model for simulation and a stronger model for writing, but the exact split was not captured in the extracted text) — UNVERIFIED.
- No STORM/Co-STORM experiment measures how many perspective-questions were already answerable without retrieval (the user's "simulated owner" filter has no analogue there).

## KQ2. Deep research agents (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, Perplexity, Elicit): plan → clarify → search → synthesize → cite; when they ask the user; reported limits

### Takeaway
All four commercial systems implement plan → (optional user review) → parallel search → synthesis with citations. Only OpenAI's product documents asking clarifying questions ("may ask"); Gemini exposes an editable plan (and, in the API, a multi-turn "collaborative planning" mode) rather than questions; Perplexity's launch page documents neither. The strongest published limitation statements are OpenAI's own (hallucination at a lower rate, inability to separate authoritative sources from rumours, poor confidence calibration) and Anthropic's (agents spawning 50 subagents, endless searching for nonexistent sources, preference for SEO content farms caught only by human testers, token cost ~15x chat).

### Cited Findings

**OpenAI Deep Research — clarifying questions and plan.** OpenAI's help centre: "Before research begins, ChatGPT may ask clarifying questions to confirm your goals. You can review and edit its proposed research plan so the report stays aligned with what you want to accomplish." — [OpenAI Help Center, "Deep research in ChatGPT"](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10500283-deep-research-in-chatgpt). The OpenAI developer forum post (28 Apr 2025) describes "a clarification request to finalize the research parameters before commencing research if anything needs clarification". — [OpenAI Forum, 2025-04-28](https://forum.openai.com/home/blogs/exploring-deep-research-three-tips-for-better-ai-assisted-inquiry-2025-04-28)

**OpenAI Deep Research — stated limitations (secondary source; openai.com returned HTTP 403 in this session).** Computing.co.uk quoting OpenAI's 2 Feb 2025 launch post: the tool "can still make things up, though at a notably lower rate than existing ChatGPT models", "may struggle with distinguishing authoritative information from rumors", and "currently shows weakness in confidence calibration, often failing to convey uncertainty accurately". — [Computing, Feb 2025](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2025/ai/openai-announces-deep-research); corroborated by [Reuters via StreetInsider](https://www.streetinsider.com/Reuters/OpenAI+launches+new+AI+tool+to+facilitate+research+tasks/24281899.html). The primary page [openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/) is UNVERIFIED here; benchmark figures (HLE 26.6%) were not confirmed from the primary source.

**Anthropic multi-agent research system (13 Jun 2025).** Architecture: orchestrator-worker; a lead agent plans and saves the plan to memory (context beyond 200k tokens is truncated), spawns subagents that search in parallel, and a separate CitationAgent attributes claims. Results: "a multi-agent system with Claude Opus 4 as the lead agent and Claude Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2% on our internal research eval"; on BrowseComp "token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance"; agents use ~4x the tokens of chat and multi-agent systems ~15x. — [Anthropic Engineering, 2025-06-13](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)

**Anthropic — failure modes stated.** Early agents "spawning 50 subagents for simple queries, scouring the web endlessly for nonexistent sources"; "small changes to the lead agent can unpredictably change subagent behavior"; errors compound in long-running stateful agents (hence checkpoints and retry); synchronous subagent execution bottlenecks; "Human testers noticed that our early agents consistently chose SEO-optimized content farms over authoritative but less highly-ranked sources" — caught by humans, not by the LLM judge; multi-agent is "a poor fit for work needing shared context, such as most coding tasks". Evaluation guidance: small test sets (~20 queries) early; LLM-as-judge with a rubric (factual accuracy, citation accuracy, completeness, source quality, tool efficiency) plus "human evaluation is still essential". — [Anthropic Engineering, 2025-06-13](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)

**Anthropic — when the lead asks the user.** The engineering post does not describe a clarifying-question step; the lead agent plans from the query. The consumer "Research" feature's clarifying behaviour is UNVERIFIED (not opened).

**Gemini Deep Research (Google AI Studio docs, agent `deep-research-preview-04-2026`).** Process "Plan → Search → Read → Iterate → Output"; optional `collaborative_planning=True` returns a plan that can be refined over several turns and then approved; "maximum research time of 60 minutes", "Most tasks should complete within 20 minutes". "The page does not say whether the agent asks clarifying questions"; the documented way to steer direction is the plan, and the guidance for missing data is to state in the prompt how unknowns should be handled (e.g., label as unavailable rather than estimate). Listed limits: no custom function tools (remote MCP only), no structured output; safety notes: verify sources via citations, hidden-text prompt injection in documents. Cost estimate $1–3 per task ($3–7 for Max). — [Google AI Studio Deep Research docs, 2026](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/deep-research). Consumer product: "Gemini drafts a research plan and shows it as a card with Edit plan and Start research buttons" — secondary guide, UNVERIFIED against Google's own page.

**Perplexity Deep Research (14 Feb 2025).** "searches, reads documents, and reasons about its next steps, refining its research plan as it learns"; "dozens of searches and hundreds of sources"; 2–4 minutes; claims 21.1% on Humanity's Last Exam and 93.9% on SimpleQA. The page does not mention clarifying questions. — [Perplexity blog, 2025-02-14](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/introducing-perplexity-deep-research)

**Elicit.** Not opened in this session — UNVERIFIED. Known publicly as a plan/search/extract/synthesise pipeline over academic papers with per-claim citations; treat any specifics as unconfirmed.

**Clarifying-question research for agents (2024–2026).**
- Zhang, Knox, Choi (ICLR 2025): train models to decide when clarification is needed by modelling future conversation turns; ~3% gain on "when to ask" judgments. — [arXiv 2410.13788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13788v2) (abstract-level, via search).
- "Active Task Disambiguation with LLMs" (Feb 2025): Bayesian experimental design scores candidate questions by expected information gain. — [arXiv 2502.04485](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/active-task-disambiguation-with-llms) (secondary summary, UNVERIFIED).
- "Ask Early, Ask Late, Ask Right" (Gulati et al., 8 May 2026): forced-injection study, 84 task variants, 6,000+ runs, four frontier models. "Goal clarification loses nearly all its value after 10% of execution, with pass@3 falling from 0.78 to baseline. Input clarification keeps its value through about 50%." "Deferring any clarification type past mid-trajectory performs worse than never asking." In 300 unscripted sessions "no current frontier model asks within the empirically optimal window. Strategies ranged from over-asking (52% of sessions) to never asking." — [arXiv 2605.07937, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07937)
- MAC (Dec 2025) states that "when to interrupt a user and how to formulate the optimal clarification query ... remain open". — [arXiv 2512.13154](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13154v1) (abstract-level).

### Inferences
- Commercial deep-research systems ask the user once, at the plan stage, and never mid-run; the "Ask Early" result explains why: goal-level questions are worth asking only in the first ~10% of execution, and late questions are worse than none. The user's loop (questions before each morph pass, human answers, then morph) is consistent with this timing evidence, provided each pass starts a new "trajectory".
- The published limitation statements are all about *synthesis* (rumours vs authority, calibration, inferential leaps) rather than about question generation; none of the vendors reports a metric for question quality.

### Gaps
- No primary-source confirmation of OpenAI's launch-post limitation wording (page returned 403; relied on Computing/Reuters quotes).
- No published data on how often OpenAI's deep research actually asks clarifying questions or how much the answers change the report.
- Elicit internals: UNVERIFIED.

## KQ3. Self-refinement loops: Self-Refine, Reflexion, Constitutional AI, evaluator-optimizer, AlphaEvolve/FunSearch, Karpathy autoresearch — loop structure, required feedback source, negative results

### Takeaway
Every loop that reports durable gains has an external, non-LLM signal in it (unit tests, environment reward, an evaluator with ground truth, a compute budget metric). Self-Refine's gains came with a strong model and specific feedback, saturated after 1–3 iterations, and were later shown (Huang; Kamoi) to depend on oracle labels or weak initial prompts; Anthropic's evaluator-optimizer guidance restricts the pattern to tasks with "clear evaluation criteria".

### Cited Findings

**Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023).** Loop: same model generates, gives feedback, refines; "iterate until a stopping condition is met ... up to a maximum of 4 iterations"; stop either at a fixed step or via a stop indicator extracted from the feedback. Reported gain: "improving by ∼20% absolute on average in task performance" over GPT-3.5/ChatGPT/GPT-4 one-step generation. Iteration analysis: "Figure 4 highlights the diminishing returns in the improvement as the number of iterations increases"; "Most gains (∆) are in the initial iterations"; "The performance may not always monotonically increase with iterations". Feedback specificity matters: replacing specific with generic feedback dropped one task from 43.2 to 31.2, and no-feedback to 24.8 on another. Weak-model failure: "Vicuna-13B was not able to consistently [generate feedback] ... either repeated the same output or generated a hallucinated conversation". Limitations (verbatim): "The main limitation of our approach is that the base models need to have sufficient few-shot modeling or instruction-following abilities, in order to learn to provide feedback and to refine in an in-context fashion". — [Madaan et al., arXiv 2303.17651, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651)

**Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023).** Actor / Evaluator / Self-Reflection with episodic memory bounded to "Ω (usually set to 1-3)" reflections. Feedback: "Reflexion converts binary or scalar feedback from the environment into verbal feedback"; evaluators are "simple binary environment feedback, pre-defined heuristics ... self-written unit tests". Results: "improves performance over strong baselines by 22% in AlfWorld, 20% in HotPotQA, and 11% on HumanEval"; HumanEval 91.0 pass@1 vs 80.1 GPT-4 baseline. Trial dynamics on AlfWorld: 12 trials; "immediate spike in the improvement between the first two trials, then a steady increase"; ReAct-only baseline "halts between trials 6 and 7". Limitations (verbatim): "it may still succumb to non-optimal local minima solutions ... Specific to code generation, there are many practical limitations to test-driven development in specifying accurate input-output mappings such as non-deterministic generator functions, impure functions that interact with APIs ...". — [Shinn et al., arXiv 2303.11366, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)

**Constitutional AI (Bai et al., Dec 2022).** Supervised phase: sample, self-critique, revise against a written constitution, fine-tune on revisions; RL phase: AI preference labels train a preference model (RLAIF). "The only human oversight is provided through a list of rules or principles." — [arXiv 2212.08073, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073). Note this is a *training* loop with the constitution as the fixed external standard, not an inference-time loop.

**Anthropic evaluator-optimizer (19 Dec 2024).** Definition: "one LLM call generates a response while another provides evaluation and feedback in a loop." When to use: "This workflow is particularly effective when we have clear evaluation criteria, and when iterative refinement provides measurable value. The two signs of good fit are, first, that LLM responses can be demonstrably improved when a human articulates their feedback; and second, that the LLM can provide such feedback." Examples: literary translation; multi-round search where the evaluator decides whether more searching is warranted. Overarching advice: "find the simplest solution possible, and only increase complexity when needed". — [Anthropic, "Building effective agents", 2024-12-19](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)

**Generator-verifier evidence (Stechly, Marquez, Kambhampati, Oct 2023).** Graph colouring: GPT-4 "is poor at solving these instances and no better at verifying them, so having an LLM critique LLM-generated solutions is ineffective"; an external sound verifier beats GPT-4-as-verifier; gains in iterative prompting "come mainly from the correct solution appearing among the model's top-k completions and being recognized by an external verifier, not from the substance of the criticisms". — [arXiv 2310.12397, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12397). Companion planning paper Valmeekam et al. 2310.08118: UNVERIFIED (not opened).

**AlphaEvolve (DeepMind, 14 May 2025).** Prompt sampler → ensemble (Gemini Flash for breadth, Pro for depth) proposes code → "automated evaluators" run and score → evolutionary programs database selects seeds. Applies to "any problem whose solution can be described as an algorithm, and automatically verified." Results: 4x4 complex matrix multiplication in 48 scalar multiplications; 0.7% of Google's worldwide compute recovered; on >50 open maths problems, matched SOTA ~75% and improved ~20%. — [DeepMind blog, 2025-05-14](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/). FunSearch (Nature, Dec 2023): same generator-plus-automatic-scorer structure — UNVERIFIED here (not opened).

**Karpathy autoresearch (March 2026).** "give an AI agent a small but real LLM training setup and let it experiment autonomously overnight." Loop: agent edits a single file `train.py`, trains for a fixed 5-minute budget, keeps or discards based on `val_bpb` (validation bits per byte, lower is better), repeats. The human's role: "you are programming the `program.md` Markdown files that provide context to the AI agents and set up your autonomous research org." Caveats: NVIDIA-only; results tied to hardware because the time budget is fixed. — [github.com/karpathy/autoresearch README](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch). Secondary reports of >700 experiments and star counts are UNVERIFIED.

**Optimal stopping (Hammar, Alpcan, Lupu; CDC 2026, submitted 11 Aug 2026).** Self-refinement "formalize[d] ... as an optimal stopping problem"; stopping policies computed by stochastic approximation are "significantly more cost-efficient than stopping policies from prior work" on a coding benchmark. The abstract gives no numbers. — [arXiv 2608.10729, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10729)

### Inferences
- The common factor across loops that work (Reflexion, AlphaEvolve, autoresearch, CAI) is a verifier that is not the generator: tests, an environment, a scorer, a constitution. Anthropic's two "signs of fit" are a restatement of the same requirement. For a document loop the only such verifier available is the human owner, which is why a human gate is load-bearing rather than optional.
- Self-Refine's own curves (most gain in iteration 1, non-monotone after) are the earliest published version of the diminishing-returns finding; later work (KQ5) sharpens it to "~one useful step without new information".

### Gaps
- FunSearch and Valmeekam et al. were not opened — UNVERIFIED.
- No published evaluation of the evaluator-optimizer pattern on documents (as opposed to code/translation) was found.

## KQ4. Self-correction without external feedback: Huang et al. 2023, Kamoi et al. 2024, and the 2026 information-theoretic follow-up

### Takeaway
Intrinsic self-correction (no oracle, no tool, no human) lowers reasoning accuracy for every model tested by Huang et al.; Kamoi et al.'s survey finds no prior work demonstrating it in general tasks under fair conditions; and Tao et al. (2026) show that one LLM reflection step carries near-zero information gain on objective tasks and negative gain on subjective ones, degrading even high-quality human first drafts.

### Cited Findings

**Huang et al. (ICLR 2024; arXiv Oct 2023).** Definition: "intrinsic self-correction ... attempts to correct its initial responses based solely on its inherent capabilities, without any external or human feedback". With oracle labels, self-correction appears to help (GPT-4 GSM8K 95.5 → 97.5), but "If we are already in possession of the ground truth, there seems to be little reason to deploy LLMs for problem-solving." Without oracle labels (Table 3/4, two rounds): GPT-4 GSM8K 95.5 → 91.5 → 89.0; GPT-4 HotpotQA 49.0 → 49.0 → 43.0; GPT-3.5 CommonSenseQA 75.8 → 38.1 → 41.8; Llama-2 GSM8K 62.0 → 43.5 → 36.5; GPT-4-Turbo GSM8K 91.5 → 88.0 → 90.0. "after self-correction, the accuracies of all models drop across all benchmarks." Mechanism: "For GSM8K, 74.7% of the time, GPT-3.5 retains its initial answer. Among the remaining instances, the model is more likely to modify a correct answer to an incorrect one than to revise an incorrect answer to a correct one. The fundamental issue is that LLMs cannot properly judge the correctness of their reasoning." Also: multi-agent debate's "efficacy is no better than self-consistency ... when considering an equivalent number of responses"; and some reported gains stem from "the sub-optimal prompt for generating initial responses". Conclusion: "expecting these models to inherently recognize and rectify their reasoning mistakes is overly optimistic so far"; "when valid external feedback is available, it is beneficial to leverage it properly". — [Huang et al., arXiv 2310.01798, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)

**Kamoi et al. (TACL 2024; arXiv Jun 2024).** Three findings (verbatim): "(1) no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs, except for studies in tasks that are exceptionally suited for self-correction, (2) self-correction works well in tasks that can use reliable external feedback, and (3) large-scale fine-tuning enables self-correction." Diagnosis: "the bottleneck is in the feedback generation". Research-design flaws catalogued: "unrealistic" settings (oracle information, e.g. RCI and Reflexion §4.2 using ground-truth stop conditions), "unfair" settings (weak initial prompts, e.g. Self-Refine's "Unfairly weak or wrong instructions or few-shot" for initial responses), and missing comparison to self-consistency at equal cost. Exception class: "tasks with specific properties that are exceptionally favorable for self-correction (e.g., responses are decomposable)", such as CoVe-style list generation. The paper supplies a checklist (Tables 7–8) for designing and for reporting negative results. — [Kamoi et al., arXiv 2406.01297, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297)

**Tao et al., Amazon, "Reflection or Re-Generation?" (arXiv 2607.28908, 31 Jul 2026, preprint under review).** Two-pass human-vs-LLM protocol with per-iteration cross-entropy reduction. "On objective reasoning tasks with finite answer spaces, reflection yields near-zero information gain (∆I ≈ 0), behaving as neutral re-generation indistinguishable from independent re-sampling. On subjective evaluation tasks, reflection produces statistically significant negative information gain (∆I < 0), consistently moving predictions away from the target. Human revision, by contrast, yields positive information gain across both settings. Cross-agent experiments localize the failure to the revision step rather than input quality: LLMs degrade even high-quality human first-pass responses." Design principles (verbatim): "1. External verification is necessary. Self-conditioned revision without new information cannot accumulate task-relevant signal ... 2. Weak–strong asymmetry can be exploited. Improvements are more likely when a stronger agent revises a weaker response ... 3. Iteration depth should be bounded. Diminishing returns across iterations (Appendix F) imply that computational resources are better allocated to independent sampling or external verification than to repeated self-conditioning." Limitations (verbatim excerpt): "restricted to tasks with finite answer spaces and reliable ground truth (MalAlgoQA and IMDb-Rating) ... Human annotators are non-expert crowdworkers ... Our second-pass prompt asks the model to agree or disagree with the prior answer, which may itself encourage revision ... part of the IMDb effect is prompt-induced disagreement while part reflects genuine drift away from the target." — [Tao et al. 2026, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28908)

### Inferences
- The three papers converge on one structural claim: an LLM re-reading its own output adds no information, so any pass of a loop that has no new input (no retrieval, no human answer, no test result) should be expected to hold or degrade quality. In the user's loop, the "morph" step is only safe because it consumes owner answers; a morph pass without new owner answers is the exact setting these papers warn about.
- The "weak–strong asymmetry" result (Tao et al.) and Self-Refine's "mixed-refine" (Vicuna init, ChatGPT feedback) both imply that if a reviewer model is used, it should be at least as strong as the drafter, and ideally a different model (see KQ6 self-preference).

### Gaps
- Tao et al. is a preprint under review; no independent replication found.
- None of these studies tests long-form documents; the closest is Tao et al.'s subjective IMDb-rating task.

## KQ5. Diminishing returns: how many refinement iterations help before quality plateaus or degrades

### Takeaway
Across the sources that report per-iteration curves, almost all of the gain arrives in iteration 1 (Self-Refine, Reflexion trial 2, Tao et al. "one revision step"), gains are non-monotone afterwards, and Huang et al.'s second round is strictly worse than the first on most benchmarks; 2026 work treats the number of iterations as an optimal-stopping problem rather than a constant.

### Cited Findings
- Self-Refine: "Most gains (∆) are in the initial iterations"; "diminishing returns in the improvement as the number of iterations increases"; "may not always monotonically increase". Max 4 iterations used. — [Madaan et al. 2023, §4, Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651)
- Reflexion AlfWorld: largest improvement between trials 1 and 2, then slow climb over 11 more trials; the non-reflective baseline stops improving at trials 6–7. — [Shinn et al. 2023, §4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)
- Huang et al.: round 2 lower than round 1 for GPT-4 on GSM8K (91.5 → 89.0) and HotpotQA (49.0 → 43.0) and for Llama-2 on GSM8K (43.5 → 36.5). — [Huang et al. 2023, Tables 3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)
- Tao et al. 2026: "Iteration depth should be bounded. Diminishing returns across iterations (Appendix F)"; a single reflection step "yields near-zero or negative information gain". — [Tao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28908)
- Hammar et al. 2026 frame iteration count as optimal stopping and report their policies are "significantly more cost-efficient" than prior stopping rules on a coding benchmark. — [arXiv 2608.10729](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10729)
- GIER (Dewri, 30 Aug 2025): gap-description-driven self-refinement on SciFact/PrivacyQA/e-SNLI with GPT-4.1, GPT-4o Mini, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Llama 3.3 70B; the abstract claims improved rationale quality and grounding "without reducing task accuracy" but does not report iteration counts. The search-result summary that "by iteration 3 most metrics plateaued" is from the full paper and is UNVERIFIED here. — [arXiv 2509.00325, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00325)
- Liang et al. force 5 rounds of debate/self-reflection to demonstrate DoT and find "the adaptive break strategy" is required for gains. — [Liang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)

### Inferences
- The defensible operating rule from the published curves is: one refinement pass per new external input, with a stopping rule tied to measured change (e.g., no accepted edits from owner answers), not a fixed number of passes.

### Gaps
- No study gives per-iteration curves for long-form document refinement with human-in-the-loop answers between passes; all curves are for QA, code, or short generation tasks.

## KQ6. LLM-as-judge biases and sycophancy / anchoring to the prompt

### Takeaway
LLM judges reach ~80% agreement with humans on chat comparisons but exhibit position, verbosity, self-enhancement (self-preference) and refinement-aware biases; position bias worsens with more candidates (robustness <0.5 at 3–4 options); self-preference persists even when sources are anonymised and is causally linked to self-recognition; and sycophancy is a general behaviour of leading assistants, partly induced by human-preference training.

### Cited Findings
- Zheng et al. (NeurIPS 2023 D&B, arXiv Jun 2023): GPT-4 judge reaches "over 80% agreement" with human preferences, "the same level of agreement between humans"; listed limitations: position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, limited reasoning ability. — [arXiv 2306.05685, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- CALM / "Justice or Prejudice?" (Ye et al., Oct 2024): 12 biases quantified (position, verbosity, self-enhancement, refinement-aware, authority, bandwagon, distraction, compassion-fade, fallacy-oversight, sentiment, diversity, CoT). "Position bias increases with more answer candidates ... particularly when evaluating three or four options, resulting in a decreased robustness rate, with most models scoring below 0.5." "Response length influences model judgment in complex ways ... Some models exhibited an aversion to excessively verbose answers, while others demonstrated a positive correlation between model preference and response length." "Avoid using the same model to generate and judge answers ... Most models rated their outputs more favorably, even when answer sources were anonymized." "LLMs show sensitivity to irrelevant content in responses." — [Ye et al., arXiv 2410.02736, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)
- Panickssery, Bowman, Feng (Apr 2024): "LLM evaluators score their own outputs higher than other texts, even though human annotators judge them to be of equal quality"; "a linear correlation between self-recognition capability and the strength of self-preference bias"; controlled experiments indicate a causal link (GPT-4, Llama 2). — [arXiv 2404.13076, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)
- Sharma et al. (Oct 2023, rev. May 2025): "five state-of-the-art AI assistants consistently exhibit sycophancy across four varied free-form text-generation tasks"; in human preference data "when a response matches a user's views, it is more likely to be preferred"; both humans and preference models sometimes prefer "convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones". — [arXiv 2310.13548, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
- Gu et al. survey (Nov 2024, rev. Oct 2025): frames reliability around "improving consistency, mitigating biases, and adapting to diverse assessment scenarios"; specific mitigations not captured from the abstract page. — [arXiv 2411.15594, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594)
- Liang et al. (MAD, 2023): "the llm-based judge shows a preference to the side with the same LLM as the backbone"; "LLM may not act as an impartial judge when different LLMs are used as debaters". — [Liang et al. 2023, §4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)
- Anthropic: LLM judge missed SEO content-farm sourcing that human testers caught. — [Anthropic 2025-06-13](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)

### Inferences
- A "simulated owner" that reads the record and decides which questions are already answered is an LLM judge with refinement-aware and self-preference exposure if it shares a model with the question generator; the CALM recommendation to split generator and judge across models applies directly.
- Sycophancy evidence implies the simulated owner will tend to confirm whatever the record implies rather than flag genuine gaps, i.e., it is biased toward cutting questions.

### Gaps
- No study specifically measures judge bias on "is this question already answered by this document" tasks.

## KQ7. Multi-agent debate, society-of-mind, role-play prompting, and review teams: do role-prompted reviewers find more distinct defects than one, or converge?

### Takeaway
Debate helps only when agents genuinely disagree (Liang's "tit for tat"; Smit's agreement modulation), otherwise it is no better than sampling the same number of answers; persona prompts alone do not improve factual accuracy (Zheng/Pei 2023; Wharton 2025) and low-knowledge personas hurt; the only study found that measures distinct-defect coverage shows that *different model families* (not personas) catch largely disjoint defects (56.5% of defects found by exactly one model), while the persona-diversity effect is only supported by blog-scale experiments.

### Cited Findings
- Du et al. (May 2023): multiple LLM instances propose, debate over rounds and converge; "society of minds" framing; reported gains in mathematical/strategic reasoning and factuality. — [arXiv 2305.14325, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325). Huang et al. (above) find this is "no better than self-consistency" at equal response count.
- Liang et al. (May 2023): Degeneration-of-Thought (verbatim): "Once the LLM-based agent has established confidence in its answers, it is unable to generate novel thoughts later through self-reflection even if the initial stance is incorrect." Causes named: "(1) Bias and Distorted Perception ... (2) Rigidity and Resistance to Change ... (3) Limited External Feedback". Findings: "the adaptive break strategy and the modest level of 'tit for tat' state are required for performance improvement"; judge favours same-backbone debater. Limitations (verbatim): "our method requires more time cost, as agents need to engage in multiple rounds of interaction ... current LLM-based agents may struggle to maintain coherence and relevance in long context scenarios". — [Liang et al., arXiv 2305.19118, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)
- Smit et al. (Nov 2023, "Should we be going MAD?"): "multi-agent debating systems, in their current form, do not reliably outperform other proposed prompting strategies, such as self-consistency and ensembling using multiple reasoning paths"; MAD protocols "are more sensitive to different hyperparameter settings and difficult to optimize"; Multi-Persona "performs consistently about 7% worse" than single agent on one dataset; prompting agents to agree "X% of the time" gave "≈15% improvement in performance for Multi-Persona, and (≈5%) for SoM on the USMLE dataset". — [Smit et al., arXiv 2311.17371, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)
- Solo Performance Prompting (Wang et al., NAACL 2024): one LLM adopts several task-specific personas and self-collaborates; "cognitive synergy emerges only in GPT-4. It does not appear in less capable models such as GPT-3.5-turbo or Llama2-13b-chat." — [arXiv 2307.05300, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05300)
- Zheng, Pei et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2024; arXiv Nov 2023): across 2,410 factual questions and 162 roles, "adding personas in system prompts does not improve model performance compared with a no-persona control"; best-persona-per-question helps but "picking it automatically was hard", with predictions "no better than random selection". — [arXiv 2311.10054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054) (abstract-level via search).
- Wharton "Playing Pretend" (Basil, Shapiro, Shapiro, Mollick, Mollick, Meincke; 5 Dec 2025): on GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro across six models, in-domain expert personas "had no significant impact on performance (with the exception of the Gemini 2.0 Flash model)"; mismatched experts sometimes lower scores or trigger refusals; low-knowledge personas ("Toddler", "Layperson") "consistently decreased performance". The authors note personas "may serve other purposes (such as altering the tone of outputs)". — [arXiv 2512.05858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05858?ref=t01.li) (abstract-level via search).
- Stone (Zenodo preprint, 3 Jun 2026): eight model versions from five providers reviewing 18 artifacts with 154 human-reconciled defects in one organisation. "No single model exceeded about 64% recall"; "56.5% of confirmed defects (87 of 154) were found by exactly one model"; median cross-family Jaccard overlap ≈0.37; "Adding a second, different-provider model raised coverage from 33.6% to 57.1%." Personas not mentioned. Caveat (verbatim): "Conclusions are directional, not a universal benchmark." — [Zenodo 20519585](https://zenodo.org/records/20519585)
- Practitioner-only persona evidence: a Substack experiment found a "cautious"/"creative" reviewer persona changed detection "at about half the magnitude of using a different model" — [clementzach.substack.com](https://clementzach.substack.com/p/improving-automated-code-review-performance) (blog, not controlled); Kosli reports two models in parallel each "wearing a set of 'persona hats'" and "genuinely catch different things" — [Kosli blog](https://www.kosli.com/blog/ai-code-review-with-specialized-llm-personas/) (engineering blog).
- "Towards Practical Defect-Focused Automated Code Review" (2025): four roles (Reviewer, Meta-Reviewer, Validator, Translator) plus code slicing and a false-alarm filter; ablation isolates multi-role reasoning but does not measure distinct-defect sets per persona. — [arXiv 2505.17928](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17928v2) (abstract-level).

### Inferences
- The evidence favours *model diversity* over *persona diversity* for finding distinct defects. Role prompts reliably change tone and focus (which may be what a question-generation step needs) but there is no controlled evidence they raise correctness, and the Wharton and Zheng/Pei results argue against expecting them to.
- "Cluster by root cause" is the step where convergence matters: if N role-reviewers share a model, their findings will be correlated (Jaccard overlap is high within a family in Stone's data), so clustering will mostly de-duplicate rather than reveal independent root causes. Mixing model families is the published lever for independent findings.
- Degeneration-of-Thought is the formal name for what a reviewer team does when every agent reads the same anchored draft: once a stance is confident, re-reading does not generate new thoughts.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed 2025–26 study was found that assigns different personas to the same model and measures distinct defect coverage in code or design review; the only such data is blog-scale.
- No study of multi-persona *document* (as opposed to code) review was found.

## KQ8. Simulated users / simulated owners: does an LLM playing a specific person predict that person?

### Takeaway
Generic persona or demographic prompting is a poor proxy for a specific human (demographic agents 74% of test-retest ceiling; Park et al.), while agents grounded in two hours of that person's own words reach 83–86% of the ceiling, with returns that "asymptote" once the model has enough in-domain evidence; in agentic settings, LLM-simulated users are measurably too polite, too forthcoming and too cooperative, inflating agent success by up to ~14 points and misattributing failures.

### Cited Findings
- Park et al. 2023 (Generative Agents): 25-agent sandbox; memory record, periodic reflection, retrieval-based planning; ablation shows "observation, planning, and reflection each contribute critically to the believability of agent behavior". Believability, not fidelity to a specific person, was the metric. — [arXiv 2304.03442, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442)
- Park et al. 2024/2025 (arXiv 2411.10109, current version titled "LLM Agents Grounded in Self-Reports Enable General-Purpose Simulation of Individuals"): 1,052 Americans; agents built from two-hour AI-conducted interviews, GSS + Big Five surveys, or both. "On held-out General Social Survey items, interview-only, survey-only, and combined agents achieved accuracies equal to 83%, 82%, and 86% of participants' own two-week test-retest consistency benchmark, respectively, compared with 74% for demographics-only agents." "gains over either source alone were modest, suggesting that predictive benefits from data begin to asymptote once the model has observed sufficient evidence within a domain." Economic games: "all agent types performed similarly and none significantly beat the demographic baseline, so we treat the games as a boundary case". Limitation (verbatim): "we do not yet fully understand why grounding agents in self-reports is so predictive, and the limits of this prediction." Conclusion: "the quality of individual simulation depends far less on model scale or synthetic persona engineering than on the depth and reliability of the data an agent is built from." — [Park et al., arXiv 2411.10109, full PDF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)
- Centaur (Binz et al., arXiv Oct 2024, rev. Apr 2025): Psych-101 (160 experiments, >60,000 participants, >10M choices); a fine-tuned language model "predicts held-out participants' behavior better than existing cognitive models" and generalises to new cover stories and domains. Population-level behaviour, not a named individual. — [arXiv 2410.20268, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20268). Nature publication status UNVERIFIED from the abstract page.
- "Mind the Sim2Real Gap" (Zhou et al., CMU, 11 Mar 2026): τ-bench with 451 real Prolific participants on 165 tasks vs 31 LLM simulators; User-Sim Index best 76.0 (DeepSeek-V3.1) vs 92.9 human inter-annotator; agent success 63.6% with humans vs up to 77.8% with general-purpose simulators. Behavioural gaps: GPT-4o polite in 49.0% of turns vs 15.3% for humans; only 1.0% short turns vs 29.0%; simulators front-load identifiers (4.8 vs 2.6 per turn); GPT-4o hedges 14.6% vs 7.3%; humans more accusatory on error while GPT-4o "pivots strategy" 19.1% vs 8.4%. Authors' framing: simulators create an "easy mode" that inflates agent success. Also: 70.6% of reward=0 interactions were judged successful by humans. Limitations: one domain family, one agent (GPT-5.2), LLM judge κ=0.6 validated on 51 interactions, US English annotators. — [arXiv 2603.11245v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11245v1)
- "Lost in Simulation" (Seshadri et al., UC Irvine/Cohere, rev. 28 Jan 2026): τ-bench retail, GPT-4o agent; swapping only the user LLM shifts agent success by ~9 points (67.0% vs 75.9%); human-user success 45.2% with ECE 15.1; simulated users ask questions in 18.8% of turns vs 9.8% for humans and use politeness markers 39.2% vs 19.9%; agents blamed for 48.9% of simulated-user failures vs 24.5% of human-user failures; calibration worst for AAVE and Indian participants (ECE 18.9). — [arXiv 2601.17087v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17087v2)
- SimulatorArena (Oct 2025): simulators "conditioned on user profiles, capturing traits like background and message styles, align closely with human judgments" — abstract-level, UNVERIFIED in detail. — [arXiv 2510.05444](https://aitopics.org/doc/arxivorg:12AEC322)
- ClarifyMT-Bench (Dec 2025) reports that clarification performance is "exacerbated under non-cooperative user personas" — abstract-level, UNVERIFIED. — [emergentmind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/clarifyagent)

### Inferences
- A "simulated owner" that answers from the record is in the best-supported regime (grounded in the person's own prior words) rather than the worst (demographic/persona prompting). Park et al. put the ceiling at roughly 83–86% of the person's own consistency when the grounding is deep; for a thin record the demographic-level figure (~74%) is the better prior.
- The Sim2Real and Lost-in-Simulation results predict the specific direction of error: the simulated owner will be more forthcoming and less ambiguous than the real owner, so it will over-answer and therefore over-cut questions, especially on questions whose real answer is "it depends" or "we haven't decided".
- Park's "asymptote once enough in-domain evidence" is the justification for the user's filter "only what the owner uniquely knows": questions in domains the record already covers are the ones a simulator can answer; questions outside it are the ones it will confabulate, so the filter must be by domain coverage of the record, not by apparent answerability.

### Gaps
- No study measures LLM prediction of a *specific expert's* answers to open design questions (all evidence is survey items, games, or customer-service dialogue).
- No evidence on how often a simulated owner's "already answered" verdict matches the real owner's verdict.

## KQ9. What these results imply for a human-gated multi-pass document loop, and what would stop such a loop from managing something other than documents

### Takeaway
The published evidence supports the loop's skeleton (role-guided questions → external answers → one morph per answer set → multi-family review) only where each pass is fed new information from outside the model; it predicts failure precisely at the steps that re-read model output without new input (simulated owner, same-model reviewers, repeated morphs). Extending the loop beyond documents requires an evaluator that can score the artefact independently of the model, which is exactly what documents lack and code, algorithms and training runs have.

### Cited Findings (synthesised from the sections above; each pointer links to the primary citation already given)

**Implications for the human-gated document loop**

1. The owner's answers are the only verifier. Every loop with durable gains has a non-LLM signal (unit tests — [Reflexion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366); automated evaluators — [AlphaEvolve](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/); val_bpb — [autoresearch](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch)); without one, self-revision is "neutral re-generation" or worse — [Tao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28908); [Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798); [Kamoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297). A morph pass with no new owner answers should be skipped, not run.
2. Perspective-guided questions buy coverage, not correctness. STORM's perspectives nearly doubled unique sources and lifted editor-rated organisation (3.25→4.00) but left verifiability flat (p=0.843) — [Shao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207). Role prompts do not raise factual accuracy — [Zheng/Pei](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054), [Wharton 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05858?ref=t01.li). Expect roles to widen the question set; do not expect them to make questions more right.
3. The simulated owner is a sycophantic judge by construction. It shares the generator's blind spots if it shares its model ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736); [Panickssery](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)), it is biased to agree with what the record implies ([Sharma](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)), and simulated users are systematically more forthcoming than real ones ([Zhou et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11245v1); [Seshadri et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17087v2)). Safeguards consistent with the evidence: use a different model family for the simulated owner than for question generation; require it to cite the exact passage that answers a question before cutting it (decomposable, verifiable sub-task — Kamoi's exception class); sample a fraction of "cut" questions to the real owner to measure the cut's false-positive rate.
4. Ask early. Goal-level clarifications lose nearly all value after the first ~10% of execution and late asking is worse than never asking — [Gulati et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07937). The loop's design of asking before each morph, rather than during it, matches this; questions discovered mid-morph should be held for the next pass.
5. Bound the passes. Gains concentrate in the first iteration and turn non-monotone after — [Self-Refine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651), [Huang et al. Tables 3–4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798), [Tao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28908); treat the pass count as a stopping problem tied to measured change ([Hammar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10729)), e.g., stop when the owner's answers produce no accepted edits.
6. Review teams converge unless models differ. Same-model reviewers degenerate ([Liang et al. DoT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)); debate equals sampling at equal cost ([Huang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798); [Smit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371)); distinct defects come from different model families (56.5% of defects found by exactly one model; a second family raised coverage 33.6%→57.1%) — [Stone 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/20519585). For "cluster by root cause" to find independent causes rather than restatements, spread role-readers across model families and have the clustering step report overlap (Jaccard) so convergence is visible.
7. Watch the synthesis step, not just the questions. STORM's dominant residual error is "improper inferential links between different pieces of information presented in the context window" needing "high-level sensemaking rather than mere fact-level verification" — [Shao et al. Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14207). The morph step that integrates many owner answers is the analogue; the human gate should read the morphed document for new unsupported connections, not only check that each answer was applied.
8. Human spot-checks catch what judges miss. Anthropic's LLM judge did not notice SEO content-farm sourcing; human testers did — [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system). Keep a human read of the review team's clusters, not only of the questions.

**What would stop such a loop from managing something other than documents**

1. Absence of an independent evaluator. Anthropic's evaluator-optimizer fit test ("clear evaluation criteria" and "iterative refinement provides measurable value") — [Anthropic 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) — is satisfied by code (tests), algorithms (scorers), and training (val_bpb) and is the reason AlphaEvolve is scoped to problems that can be "automatically verified" — [DeepMind 2025](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/). For artefacts where the only evaluator is a human opinion (strategy, policy, design intent), the loop cannot run unattended; the human gate is not removable.
2. Statefulness and irreversibility. Errors compound in long-running stateful agents ([Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)); late clarification "performs worse than never asking" once irreversible actions have been taken ([Gulati et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07937)). Documents are cheap to revert; infrastructure, money, and sent communications are not, so the question-then-morph cadence would need checkpointing and dry-run execution to transfer.
3. Shared-context tasks. Anthropic explicitly says multi-agent decomposition is "a poor fit for work needing shared context, such as most coding tasks" — [Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system); a role-reader review team on a tightly coupled codebase would produce correlated, context-starved findings.
4. Simulated-user fidelity collapses outside the record. Agent success is inflated by 9–14 points under simulated users and failures are misattributed ([Zhou et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11245v1); [Seshadri et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17087v2)); grounding only works "once the model has observed sufficient evidence within a domain" ([Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)). Managing a process whose owner's preferences are not already in writing would push the simulated owner into the demographic-persona regime (~74% of ceiling or worse).
5. Cost scaling. Multi-agent research runs at ~15x chat token cost and token use explains ~80% of performance variance ([Anthropic 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)); debate "requires more time cost" ([Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118)). A loop applied to many small operational decisions rather than a few documents inverts the value/cost ratio Anthropic says must justify multi-agent use.
6. Judge bias scales with candidate count. Position-bias robustness falls below 0.5 at 3–4 candidates ([CALM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)); a loop ranking many alternatives (tickets, options, vendors) rather than editing one document would hit this directly.

### Inferences
- The loop is best read as a human-amplification device: its published analogues (STORM for pre-writing, Co-STORM for unknown unknowns, OpenAI/Gemini plan review) all leave the final judgement to a person, and the negative results all describe what happens when that person is replaced by the model.
- The single cheapest instrumentation implied by the evidence is to log, per pass, (a) how many questions the simulated owner cut and how many of a sampled subset the real owner would have answered differently, and (b) the overlap between role-reviewers' clusters; these two numbers are the loop's analogues of the "oracle label" and "self-consistency baseline" that Huang and Kamoi say every self-correction claim must be checked against.

### Gaps
- No published system combines a simulated-user filter with a human answer gate on a document, so the compound error (simulator over-cuts, then human never sees the cut question) is unmeasured.
- No published evidence on root-cause clustering of multi-agent review findings.
- Primary pages not opened this session (UNVERIFIED): openai.com deep-research launch post (403), Elicit documentation, FunSearch (Nature 2023), Valmeekam et al. 2310.08118, GIER full text, Gemini consumer plan-card behaviour, Anthropic consumer Research clarifying behaviour, Centaur's Nature publication.
